A start-to-finish guide for taking a whitetail from the field to the freezer: tagging and reporting in Virginia, field dressing, cooling, aging, breaking the deer down, and cooking the cuts. Pair it with [[Meat Cooking Temps by Cut]] when you are ready to cook.

> [!warning]
> Hunting rules change every year and vary by county and Disease Management Area. Always check the current Virginia DWR regulations before you hunt. This guide is a working reference, not a substitute for them.

## At a Glance

| | |
|---|---|
| First priority | Tag, report, then cool the meat fast |
| Cooling target | Below 40 F as soon as possible |
| Aging | 32 to 38 F for 2 to 3 days, hide on, when you have the cooler space |
| Ground venison | Cook to 160 F internal |
| Whole cuts | 145 F internal with a rest, per USDA guidance |
| Freezing | Wrapped airtight, at 0 F |

## Step 1: Tag and Report in Virginia

- **Validate your tag at the kill site, before you move the deer.** You can do this electronically with the GoOutdoorsVirginia app (eNotch) or by removing the notch on a paper tag.
- **Report the harvest** when the carcass is moved by vehicle or at the end of legal hunting hours, whichever comes first, and without unnecessary delay. You can report online at GoOutdoorsVirginia.com, through the app, or by phone at 866-468-4263.
- **Write down the 8-digit confirmation number** on your paper tag or personal check card.
- **Do not destroy the identity (sex) of the animal until it is reported.**
- After tagging, you may cut up the carcass for transport, but every part except the internal organs must stay with the animal until it is reported.
- If the carcass is left unattended or handed to someone else, the personal check card must stay with it.

## Step 2: After the Shot

1. Stay put for a while (30 minutes is a common rule of thumb for a well-placed shot) unless you saw the deer fall.
2. Mark where the deer was standing and any sign you find: blood, hair, and direction of travel.
3. Follow the trail slowly and quietly. Mark each spot of blood with tape or a light.
4. Tag it as soon as you reach the deer, then note the time so you know how long the meat has been warm.

## Step 3: Field Dressing

The goal is to get the internal organs out fast without puncturing the intestines or bladder and to get the carcass cooling quickly.

### Traditional method

1. Lay the deer on its back with the head uphill if possible.
2. Cut around the anus and free the lower end of the intestine, then tie it off or cover it with a bag to keep it clean.
3. Make a shallow cut through the hide from the pelvis to the bottom of the breastbone, using two fingers to lift the skin and push the organs away from the blade.
4. Cut through the diaphragm along the rib cage, then reach up and cut the windpipe and esophagus as high as you can.
5. Roll the organs out of the cavity, freeing them along the spine.
6. Wipe the cavity dry with a towel or cloth. Avoid rinsing with pond or stream water, which adds bacteria.
7. Prop the chest open with a clean stick so air can get in and heat can get out.

### Gutless method

- The gutless method skips opening the body cavity. You skin the deer and remove the quarters, backstraps, and tenderloins in pieces.
- It is popular because it keeps meat clean and makes it easier to pack out from a distance.
- It also limits handling of the head, spine, and organs, which is why Virginia DWR recommends it for CWD-related handling.

> [!note]
> Wear gloves the whole time. Do not eat or drink while you work, and wash your hands and tools well afterward.

## Step 4: Cool the Meat

- The meat should be **below 40 F as soon as possible**. Heat and time are the main enemies of good venison.
- In cool weather (below 40 F), hang the deer with the cavity open and let air circulate.
- In warm weather, get the deer to a cooler quickly, or use bags of ice in the chest cavity for the trip out.
- If you plan to process right away, skin, quarter, and bag the meat, then put it on ice or in a cooler until you are ready to trim.

## Step 5: Hanging and Aging

- If you have access to a walk-in cooler, age the whole carcass at **32 to 38 F for 2 to 3 days** with the hide on. The hide protects the meat from drying out and shrinkage.
- If you do not have a cooler, do not age it at ambient temperature. Skin, quarter, and refrigerate the meat instead, where it can rest in game bags or on a rack in a cooler with drained ice.
- Aging helps tenderness, but it is not worth risking spoilage. Cold and clean matter far more than extra days.

## Step 6: Skinning and Breaking Down

Work on a clean surface and keep everything cold. Keep the meat as cool as possible between steps.

1. **Skin the deer.** Hanging it makes this much easier. Pull hide away from the meat with your hands and use the knife only where you need to cut connective tissue.
2. **Take the backstraps** (loins) by cutting along the spine from the neck to the hip, then peeling the meat away from the spine and ribs.
3. **Take the tenderloins** from inside the body cavity along the spine, if you dressed the deer traditionally.
4. **Remove the front shoulders.** They are not attached to the skeleton by bone, so cut the connective tissue and lift them off.
5. **Remove the hindquarters** by following the natural seams and separating at the hip joint.
6. **Take the neck meat and trim from the ribs** for grinding or stew.
7. **Cut the rib meat** if you want it, or leave the ribs for stock.

## Step 7: Trim Well

- Remove all the fat, silverskin, tendon, and bloodshot meat. Venison fat turns waxy and gamey in the freezer and is the main source of "wild" flavor.
- Keep whole muscles intact where you can. Smaller trim goes to the grind pile.
- Bloodshot meat or anything contaminated with hair or gut contents goes in the discard pile. When in doubt, throw it out.

## Cuts and How to Use Them

| Cut | Best for | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Tenderloin | Fast searing, grilling | The most tender cut, cook it hot and quick |
| Backstrap (loin) | Steaks, medallions, grill, cast iron | Slice against the grain, do not overcook |
| Hindquarter roasts (round, sirloin tip) | Roasts, jerky, steaks, kabobs | Trim silverskin, slice thin for jerky |
| Shoulder | Slow cooking, braising, stew, pulled venison | Lots of connective tissue, needs low and slow |
| Shanks | Braising, osso buco style | Slow, moist heat |
| Neck | Slow cooker, stew, pot roast | A great braising cut |
| Ribs | Slow braising or stock | Often not worth the effort on a small deer |
| Trim | Ground venison, sausage, burger | Add fat (beef suet or pork) if you want juicier burgers |

## Step 8: Packaging and Freezing

- Wrap meat tightly to push out air. Vacuum sealing is the best method. Otherwise, use freezer paper over plastic wrap.
- Label every package with the cut, date, and the year.
- Freeze at 0 F. Use ground venison and lean cuts within about 9 to 12 months for best quality.
- Portion sizes matter. Wrap steaks and roasts in meal-sized packages so you can thaw only what you need.

## Step 9: Cooking Safely

- **Ground venison:** cook to 160 F internal.
- **Whole cuts (steaks, roasts):** USDA guidance is 145 F with a three minute rest. Many hunters prefer backstrap and tenderloin cooked more rare. That is a personal choice, and safe handling from field to kitchen makes a big difference.
- **Slow-cooked cuts:** braise until tender. Shoulder, neck, and shank are ready when they pull apart easily.
- Use a thermometer. See [[Meat Cooking Temps by Cut]] for a quick reference.

## Chronic Wasting Disease (CWD)

CWD is a fatal disease of deer that is present in parts of Virginia, and the state runs Disease Management Areas (DMAs) with special rules.

- Check the current DMA map and rules before you hunt and before you move any carcass.
- In some areas, hunters are asked or required to bring harvested deer to DWR sampling stations so lymph nodes can be tested. This normally means the head with 3 to 4 inches of neck.
- Do not shoot, handle, or eat an animal that looks sick.
- Wear gloves when field dressing, and minimize contact with the brain, spinal cord, spleen, and lymph nodes.
- In DMAs, high-risk parts (head, brain, spinal cord, spleen, and brain-tanned hides) cannot be moved out of the area. Boneless meat, cleaned hides without heads, cleaned skull plates and antlers, and finished taxidermy mounts are the standard safe items to transport.
- For interstate transport of deer, plan on bringing home only caped, skulled, or processed venison.
- If your deer tests positive, do not eat it. Talk to DWR about disposal.

## Notes

- Keep meat clean and cold. That single habit does more for the flavor than any marinade.
- Wait to grind until after you have trimmed everything properly.
- Have a plan for where the deer will go before you shoot. Knowing where your cooler, your knives, and your freezer space are makes the whole day smoother.
- Take photos for the memory, but keep the tag and the report first on your checklist.
- Keep a simple log of each deer: date, location, weather, weight, and how the meat turned out. Over the years it becomes a useful record.
